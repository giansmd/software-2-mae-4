# Analysis of the problem

## From model to digital twin

The conceptual distinction between a model and a digital twin is foundational to this work. Wright and Davidson (2020) argue that the difference lies not in the sophistication of the underlying model but in the coupling: while a conventional model represents a system in a static or unidirectional manner, a digital twin maintains a bidirectional link with its physical counterpart, continuously updating itself from observed data [3].

Rasheed et al. (2020), after reviewing the literature, propose an operative definition: a digital twin is a virtual representation of a physical asset enabled by data and simulators for prediction, optimisation and real-time monitoring [1]. Verdouw et al. (2021) emphasise the temporal dimension by describing it as a dynamic representation of a real object that mirrors its states and behaviour throughout its life cycle [2]. Both formulations converge on three constitutive elements: a physical counterpart, a computational model and a data flow that keeps them synchronised.

Durão et al. (2018) systematise the requirements that the literature imposes on the concept, establishing that its industrial adoption depends on the capacity to represent not only the geometry and structure of the asset, but also its operative behaviour under changing conditions [18]. This requirement translates directly to the environmental domain: a digital twin of an ecosystem must represent its dynamics, not merely its state.

## Architectures and implementation frameworks

Building digital twins at ecosystem scale requires architectures capable of integrating heterogeneous sources. Nativi et al. (2021) describe the digital ecosystem developed within the Destination Earth initiative, and argue that a comprehensive discussion of the multiple dimensions and challenges of digital ecosystems fills an important gap in current knowledge [19].

In the technical implementation domain, Picone et al. (2023) propose a flexible and modular architecture for edge digital twins, oriented towards reducing latency through local data processing [20]. Raes et al. (2022) present the DUET framework, aimed at building interoperable and trustworthy digital twins for smart cities, addressing the need for systems developed by different actors to exchange information [21]. Interoperability, they note, is a condition for avoiding fragmentation into data silos.

Chen et al. (2025) address the problem of federated digital twin construction via distributed sensing, proposing an online optimisation scheme based on game theory. They warn, however, that the number of sensors associated with each entity is limited and that the accumulated cost is constrained by that restriction [22]. This observation is relevant to any environmental deployment, where instrumentation resources are inherently bounded.

At ecosystem scale, Khan et al. (2025) propose TwinEco, a unified framework for dynamic data-driven digital twins in ecology, with design principles applicable to urban, agricultural and natural ecosystems [23]. Qamsane et al. (2021) contribute a structured methodology for developing and implementing digital twin solutions, with explicit phases of scope definition, modelling and validation [16]. This methodological structure parallels the CRISP-DM approach adopted in the present work.

## Modelling, emulation and informed physics

The tension between physical fidelity and computational cost defines much of the research in this field. Jiang et al. (2021) developed the first digital twin of the Earth's coasts, employing physics-informed neural operators as surrogates for deterministic coastal flood models. Their emulator predicts sea surface height accurately and achieves speed-ups of up to forty-five times relative to the reference numerical model, enabling the efficient execution of multiple scenarios for decision-making and uncertainty quantification [6].

Yang et al. (2024) examine physics-informed neural networks from the digital twin perspective, and note that many approaches restrict their scope to the use of sparse guide data —between one and ten percent of the mesh points—, which constitutes an inefficient exploitation of available simulation results [24]. Zia et al. (2025) propose the incorporation of physics-encoded blocks into residual network architectures, and warn that operational datasets commonly present missing values originating from power cuts, sensor failures or adverse weather conditions [25]. Dilip (2024) applies the same principle to asphalt pavements, building a thermal digital twin via physics-informed neural networks [26].

Reichstein et al. (2019) frame this convergence in a broader context: deep learning and process understanding must be integrated for the advancement of data-driven Earth system science, overcoming the opposition between purely statistical and purely mechanistic approaches [27].

## Data, sensors and uncertainty

The quality of a digital twin is bounded by the quality of its data. Guo et al. (2017) review wetland remote sensing and document that aerial photography offers advantages in spatial resolution and acquisition time, although its use is usually restricted to narrow coastal areas or riverbanks due to limited coverage [11]. Jafarzadeh et al. (2022), through a meta-analysis of three decades, synthesise established practices for wetland monitoring and highlight that the findings are essential for determining best practices in environmental management, restoration and conservation [12].

Biases in ecological data constitute a structural limitation. Meyer et al. (2015) establish global priorities for an effective information basis of biodiversity distributions [28], and Meyer et al. (2016) demonstrate that the widespread application of occurrence information is limited by gaps, uncertainties and biases in the taxonomic, spatial and temporal dimensions [13]. Mollenhauer et al. (2018) evaluate the geographical representativeness of long-term environmental monitoring infrastructures in Europe and conclude that a critical gap persists in the research infrastructure landscape [14]. Jeffery et al. (2021) extend the warning to the plane of data infrastructures, arguing that convergence between repositories and systems is not yet resolved [29].

Uncertainty must therefore be propagated explicitly. Buffington et al. (2021) incorporate uncertainty into projections of tidal wetland elevation and carbon accumulation under sea-level rise scenarios, and demonstrate that both salinity scenarios and productivity functions are determinant in projecting how carbon capture may change [15]. Cabrera et al. (2021), when evaluating the potential of smartphones as urban weather stations, warn that their results represent the behaviour of a single device model, which illustrates the need to bound the scope of conclusions to the effectively observed domain [30].

## Environmental applications and governance

The recent literature insists that the value of an environmental digital twin is decided by its usefulness for decision-making, not by its technical sophistication. Durden (2025) identifies four main challenges that condition the generation of products meaningful to user needs and argues that the development of use cases must focus explicitly on those outcomes [17]. The author further notes that, although decision-makers are the most evident users, the incorporation of other stakeholders broadens the capacity for collaborative decision-making and communication [17].

The quality of the data available for these systems depends in part on non-professional sources. Brown and Williams (2019) argue that hypothesis testing in the confirmatory paradigm is entirely possible with large-scale internet-based citizen science projects, provided they are specifically designed for that purpose, although they note that such projects are scarce [31]. This observation broadens the universe of data potentially integrable into an ecological digital twin, while warning of the requirement for deliberate methodological design for such observations to be usable.

De Koning et al. (2023) warn that the adoption of the concept in ecology must be accompanied by a frank discussion of its inherent limitations, to avoid expectations that may mislead modellers, researchers, policy-makers and natural resource managers [5]. Tzachor et al. (2023) extend that warning to the oceanic domain, anticipating ethical concerns and uncertainties in the digital representation of marine ecosystem services [7].

In the governance domain, Haraguchi et al. (2024) examine both the benefits and limitations of urban digital twins, exploring the specific areas where their impact is effective and those where it is bounded [32]. Hananto and Veza (2025) propose a governance framework for intelligent digital twin systems oriented towards aligning standards and practices [33], while DeVries (2024) addresses the privacy and data sovereignty challenges that emerge from integrating these technologies into urban ecosystems [34]. Garske et al. (2024) analyse the role of European data governance in the sustainable transition [35].

Specific applications illustrate the range of the paradigm. Henriksen et al. (2023) develop a digital twin for climate change adaptation, water management and disaster risk reduction, noting that the depth of the water table is the determining variable of carbon emissions in peat soils, and that knowledge of hydrological variability is decisive for guiding the restoration of drained peatlands [36]. Rodríguez-Alonso et al. (2024) present a digital twin platform for water treatment plants based on a microservices architecture, oriented towards optimising operation and maintenance through machine learning and simulation [37]. Alenezi and Alabaiadly (2026) review the application of digital twins and edge computing to small off-grid water systems [38]. Verdouw et al. (2021) document their adoption in smart farming, noting that studies in that domain are relatively scarce compared with other sectors [2].

## Coastal wetlands and blue carbon

Coastal wetlands constitute a case of application with its own characteristics. Twomey et al. (2024) argue that restoration oriented towards blue carbon provides renewed impetus to the recovery of hydrologically modified coastal plains, but warn that guidance for hydrological restoration remains limited and is frequently focused on particular regions, activities or risks [10]. They further stress that ecosystem functions and processes influenced by the hydrological regime determine services such as habitat provision, water quality improvement, carbon capture and coastal protection [10].

The transferability of models is an explicitly recognised limitation. Twomey et al. (2024) note that models may not be transferable to other sites and that their predictive capacity is restricted to the specific conditions of the data used in their construction [10]. In relation to sea-level rise, they identify that uncertainty in elevation data and in soil surface changes over time are key limitations for many models, despite being determining parameters for projecting impacts on restoration sites [10].

Lu et al. (2026) explore specifically the concept of wetland digital twins for ecosystem monitoring and management, noting that the state and dynamics of the ecosystem under future scenarios are essential for stakeholders to carry out proactive management oriented towards conserving the ecosystem and supporting its restoration [9]. They also observe that wetlands have suffered significant losses and degradation at a global scale over the last centuries due to human disturbances and climate change [9].

## The CRISP-DM methodology

CRISP-DM —*Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining*— organises an analytical project into six iterative phases: business understanding, data understanding, preparation, modelling, evaluation and deployment. Unlike approaches centred exclusively on the algorithm, the standard demands that every analytical artefact be traceable to an explicit business objective and that evaluation contemplate success criteria defined beforehand.

The methodology parallels the structured frameworks proposed for digital twin development. Qamsane et al. (2021) establish explicit phases of scope definition, modelling and validation in their implementation methodology [16], and Durden (2025) insists that use-case development must start from user needs and not from available technical capabilities [17]. The present work adopts the CRISP-DM framework and documents each phase with its verifiable products.
