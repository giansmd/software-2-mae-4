# Introduction

Digital twins emerged in the engineering domain as virtual representations of a physical asset, continuously updated to reflect its current structure and behaviour [1]. Their canonical definition describes them as a virtual representation of a physical asset enabled by data and simulators for prediction, optimisation and real-time monitoring [1], or as a dynamic representation of a real object that mirrors its states and behaviour throughout its life cycle [2]. The distinction from a conventional model is not trivial: it lies in the bidirectional coupling between the physical system and its virtual counterpart, which confers on the latter the capacity for permanent updating [3].

Applying this paradigm to natural systems is a recent, far-reaching turn. Blair (2021) argues that digital twins of the natural environment represent a singular opportunity, but warns that their construction demands combining the inherited understanding of process-based models with new data-analysis and machine-learning approaches [4]. Ecology has begun to adopt the concept as a dynamic fusion of models and data, although inherent limitations persist that should be made explicit so as not to generate exaggerated expectations among modellers, researchers and policy-makers [5].

In aquatic and coastal ecosystems, interest is growing. The Digital Twin Earth initiative has developed emulators based on physics-informed neural operators to predict sea levels in coastal regions, achieving speed-ups of over forty times relative to deterministic numerical models [6]. Tzachor et al. (2023) argue that digital twins may constitute a stepping stone towards ocean sustainability, while warning of the ethical risks and uncertainties of digitally representing marine ecosystem services [7]. Aguzzi et al. (2025) demonstrate that robotic platforms can generate high-resolution marine monitoring data, but note persisting challenges in spatial coverage, temporal continuity and ecological representativeness [8].

Wetlands, and mangrove ecosystems in particular, are an especially pertinent case. Lu et al. (2026) explore the concept of wetland digital twins, pointing out that the ecosystem's state and dynamics under future scenarios are essential for proactive management that conserves ecosystems and supports their restoration [9]. Hydrological restoration of coastal wetlands has received renewed impetus from blue carbon: Twomey et al. (2024) warn that hydrologically influenced ecosystem functions determine services such as habitat, water quality, carbon capture and coastal protection, yet guidance for hydrological restoration remains limited and frequently restricted to particular regions or risks [10]. Existing models also face a transferability limitation: their predictive capacities are confined to the specific conditions of the data from which they were built [10].

Remote sensing and machine learning have substantially expanded monitoring capacity. Guo et al. (2017) review wetland remote-sensing research —classification, biodiversity, biomass estimation, water quality, mangrove forest, sea-level rise— [11], and Jafarzadeh et al. (2022), in a three-decade meta-analysis, synthesise best practices for wetland management, restoration and conservation from combined remote sensing and machine learning [12]. Systematic biases persist, however: Meyer et al. (2016) show that species occurrence information is limited by gaps, uncertainties and biases in the taxonomic, spatial and temporal dimensions [13], and Mollenhauer et al. (2018) identify the geographical representativeness of long-term monitoring infrastructures as a critical gap in the European research landscape [14]. The associated uncertainty deserves explicit attention: Buffington et al. (2021) incorporate uncertainty into projections of tidal wetland elevation and carbon accumulation, demonstrating that salinity scenarios and productivity functions are determinant for projecting carbon capture [15] — directly relevant to any system aspiring to inform restoration decisions.

On this basis, the present work addresses three concrete gaps. First, documented ecological digital twins concentrate on marine, urban or agricultural ecosystems, with scarce attention to the mangrove coastal wetlands of the southeastern Pacific. Second, the hydrological restoration literature explicitly recognises the absence of transferable guidance and the dependence of projections on local calibration conditions [10]. Third, existing implementations rarely make explicit the separation between the empirical and simulated portions of their data, hindering audit of the basis of their results.

To address them, a digital twin of the Los Manglares de Tumbes National Sanctuary (SNLMT), in northern Peru, was built, applying the CRISP-DM methodology to structure the analytical process into six traceable phases: business understanding, data understanding, preparation, modelling, evaluation and deployment [16]. The working hypothesis postulates that hydrological restoration can increase blue carbon retention by at least 25 % by 2050 relative to baseline.

The contribution is threefold. First, a hybrid dataset with explicit traceability of its real and simulated components is documented, anchored in open literature on the SNLMT. Second, five algorithms are compared under an evaluation protocol combining cross-validation, statistical significance testing and bootstrap resampling, following established practice for rigorous model comparison [5]. Third, an interactive dashboard is delivered that exposes interpretability as a first-class capability, heeding the recommendation to centre environmental digital twin development on outcomes meaningful to users rather than isolated technical capabilities [17].

Development was sustained on a distributed version control repository accumulating 39 commits between 31 August and 29 September 2026. This versioning discipline is not an instrumental detail: every quality review finding, correction and feature addition was recorded with a descriptive message allowing the system's evolution to be reconstructed and the origin of any design decision to be audited. Issue planning on a tracking platform was projected as a complement, although its full implementation exceeded the deliverable's time scope.
