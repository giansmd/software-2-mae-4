# Introduction

Digital twins emerged in the engineering domain as virtual representations of a physical asset, continuously updated to reflect its current structure and behaviour [1]. Their canonical definition describes them as a virtual representation of a physical asset enabled by data and simulators for prediction, optimisation and real-time monitoring [1], or as a dynamic representation of a real object that mirrors its states and behaviour throughout its life cycle [2]. The distinction between a conventional model and a digital twin is not trivial: it lies in the bidirectional coupling between the physical system and its virtual counterpart, which confers on the latter the capacity for permanent updating [3].

The application of this paradigm to natural systems is a recent and far-reaching turn. Blair (2021) argues that digital twins of the natural environment represent a singular opportunity, but warns that their construction demands combining the inherited understanding of process-based models with new data-analysis and machine-learning approaches [4]. Ecology has begun to adopt the concept as a form of dynamic fusion between models and data, although inherent limitations persist that should be made explicit so as not to generate exaggerated expectations among modellers, researchers and policy-makers [5].

In the realm of aquatic and coastal ecosystems, interest is growing. The Digital Twin Earth initiative has developed emulators based on physics-informed neural operators to predict sea levels in coastal regions, achieving speed-ups of over forty times relative to deterministic numerical models [6]. Tzachor et al. (2023) argue that digital twins may constitute a stepping stone towards ocean sustainability, while warning of the ethical risks and uncertainties associated with the digital representation of marine ecosystem services [7]. Aguzzi et al. (2025) demonstrate that robotic platforms can generate high-resolution data for marine monitoring, but note that challenges remain in achieving adequate spatial coverage, temporal continuity and ecological representativeness [8].

Wetlands, and in particular mangrove ecosystems, are an especially pertinent case of application. Lu et al. (2026) explore the concept of wetland digital twins and point out that the state of the ecosystem and its dynamics under future scenarios are essential for stakeholders to carry out proactive management that conserves ecosystems and supports their restoration [9]. Hydrological restoration of coastal wetlands, specifically, has received renewed impetus from blue carbon: Twomey et al. (2024) warn that ecosystem functions and processes influenced by the hydrological regime determine services such as habitat, water quality, carbon capture and coastal protection, but that guidance for hydrological restoration remains limited and is frequently restricted to particular regions or risks [10]. Existing models also face a transferability limitation: their predictive capacities are confined to the specific conditions of the data from which they were built [10].

Remote sensing and machine learning have substantially expanded the monitoring capacity of these ecosystems. Guo et al. (2017) review research topics in wetland remote sensing —classification, biodiversity, biomass estimation, water quality, mangrove forest and sea-level rise— and document the progress of available techniques [11]. Jafarzadeh et al. (2022), in a meta-analysis of three decades, synthesise best practices for environmental management, restoration and conservation of wetlands based on the combination of remote sensing and machine learning [12]. Systematic biases and gaps persist, however: Meyer et al. (2016) demonstrate that the widespread application of species occurrence information is limited by gaps, uncertainties and biases in the taxonomic, spatial and temporal dimensions [13], and Mollenhauer et al. (2018) show that the geographical representativeness of long-term environmental monitoring infrastructures constitutes a critical gap in the European research landscape [14].

The uncertainty associated with these projections deserves explicit attention. Buffington et al. (2021) incorporate uncertainty into projections of tidal wetland elevation and carbon accumulation under sea-level rise scenarios, demonstrating that both salinity scenarios and productivity functions are determinant for projecting how carbon capture may change [15]. This finding is directly relevant to any system that aspires to inform restoration decisions.

On the basis of these antecedents, the present work addresses three concrete gaps. First, documented ecological digital twins concentrate on marine, urban or agricultural ecosystems, with scarce attention to the mangrove coastal wetlands of the southeastern Pacific. Second, the literature on hydrological restoration of coastal wetlands explicitly recognises the absence of transferable guidance and the dependence of projections on local calibration conditions [10]. Third, existing implementations rarely make explicit the separation between the empirical and the simulated portions of their data, which makes it difficult to audit the basis of their results.

To address them, a digital twin of the Los Manglares de Tumbes National Sanctuary (SNLMT), in northern Peru, was built, and the CRISP-DM methodology was applied to structure the analytical process into six traceable phases: business understanding, data understanding, preparation, modelling, evaluation and deployment [16]. The working hypothesis postulates that hydrological restoration can increase blue carbon retention by at least 25 % by 2050 relative to the baseline.

The contribution of the work is threefold. First, a hybrid dataset with explicit traceability of its real and simulated components is documented, anchored in open literature on the SNLMT. Second, five algorithms are compared under an evaluation protocol combining cross-validation, statistical significance testing and bootstrap resampling, following the established practice for rigorous model comparison [5]. Third, an interactive dashboard is delivered that exposes interpretability as a first-class capability, heeding the recommendation to centre the development of environmental digital twins on outcomes meaningful to users rather than on isolated technical capabilities [17].

Development was sustained on a distributed version control repository that accumulated 39 commits between 31 August and 29 September 2026. This versioning discipline is not an instrumental detail: every quality review finding, every correction and every feature addition was recorded with a descriptive message that allows the evolution of the system to be reconstructed and the origin of any design decision to be audited. Issue planning on a tracking platform was projected as a complement, although its full implementation exceeded the time scope of the deliverable.
